A practical question

What happens to your accounts when you die?

Your family may know you had a bank account, a house, and a phone. That doesn't mean they know where everything is, what gets paid automatically, or what needs attention first.

First, someone has to find everything

After a death, the practical work begins while people are grieving. Someone has to identify accounts, recurring bills, insurance, property, important records, subscriptions, devices, and the people who need to be contacted.

Some of those things are obvious. Others are buried in email, saved passwords, paper files, an app, a desk drawer, or one person's memory.

The hard part is often information, not paperwork.
Legal and financial professionals may handle the parts that require their expertise. The family still needs to know what exists, where to look, and who to contact.

What families commonly need to locate

  • Bank and investment accounts
  • Credit cards, loans, and recurring payments
  • Life, health, home, auto, and other insurance information
  • Mortgage, lease, property, vehicle, and household information
  • Utilities, subscriptions, memberships, and automatic payments
  • Important records and documents
  • Phones, computers, password managers, email, and other digital accounts
  • Business information when the person owns or operates a company

Automatic payments don't know you're grieving

Mortgage payments, utilities, insurance premiums, subscriptions, software, memberships, and other charges can continue according to their own schedules. Knowing what exists gives the person handling the transition a starting point.

The exact treatment of an account after death depends on the account, ownership, beneficiary designations, contracts, and applicable law. This page is about organizing information, not providing legal or financial advice.

The question most families actually need answered

Before anything else, someone needs a reliable answer to: Where do I look?

A will or trust can establish important legal instructions. A continuity guide can document the practical map around those documents, including accounts, contacts, records, household information, digital life, and instructions.

Make the first 72 hours easier to navigate

You don't need to predict every problem. You need to make the important information findable by the person who may have to step in.

That is one reason a Life Manual exists. It gives the people who may need to act a single place to start, while you are still here to explain what matters and why.
