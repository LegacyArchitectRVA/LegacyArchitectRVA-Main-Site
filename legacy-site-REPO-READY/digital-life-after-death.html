Your digital life has a life of its own

What Happens to Your Digital Life When You Die?

Email, photos, social media, passwords, subscriptions, files, and devices can become someone else's problem unless you've made the path clear.

Your digital life doesn't come with a single key

Most of us have built a second life online without really thinking about it. Email accounts connect to financial accounts. Phones hold authentication codes. Cloud storage holds family photos. Password managers hold the keys to everything else.

When you're gone, someone may need to identify those accounts, understand what matters, and follow each provider's process. Access rules vary by service and jurisdiction, so having a password alone does not automatically give another person legal authority to access an account.

The goal is a map, not a pile of passwords.
Someone stepping in needs to know what exists, where the information is kept, which accounts matter, and what you want done with them.

What belongs on the digital-life map?

  • Primary email and recovery email accounts
  • Phones, computers, tablets, and other connected devices
  • Password manager and emergency-access instructions
  • Cloud storage, photographs, documents, and backups
  • Social media and messaging accounts
  • Streaming, shopping, membership, and subscription accounts
  • Domains, websites, online businesses, and digital workspaces
  • Digital assets and files that other people may need to locate

Passwords are only one piece of the problem

A family member may know your phone passcode and still have no idea which email address controls your important accounts. They may find your password manager but not know which accounts should be preserved, closed, transferred, or reviewed first.

That is why digital continuity starts with organization and instructions. The exact access method should follow the rules of the services involved and the legal authority available to the person handling your affairs.

Think about the things you would hate to lose

Photos. Messages. Family history. A website you built. A domain you've owned for years. Files nobody else knows exist. The subscription you forgot about. The account connected to three other accounts.

Some digital things have financial value. Others have personal value. Both deserve a place on the map.

Give someone a place to start

A Life Manual can document the practical side of your digital life alongside financial information, household operations, vital records, contacts, and the decisions someone may need to understand if you are unavailable.

The point is simple: when someone else has to step in, they should be able to see the path instead of reconstructing your digital life from scratch.

Would they know where your digital life begins?
